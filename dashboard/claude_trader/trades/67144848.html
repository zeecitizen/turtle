Why it didn't make money

1. No trend at all

The 9 and 50 EMAs were 53 cents apart — flat and tangled. The EA's whole idea is a rejection off an EMA in a move; here there was no move to rejoin. The chop reading (0.111) passed the 0.11 limit by a hair.

2. The second buy in 90 minutes at the same area

67138222 had been stopped out from 4169 at 04:06. This trade bought 4166.69 forty minutes later, into the same overhead area, and failed the same way. The EA has a cooldown setting after a loss (InpCoolBars), but it's off by default.

3. No target

As in 67138222: exit EMA below the entry, so no TP1 and no breakeven step — the trade could only be saved by a trail that starts a candle later.

The size: 2 lots on a $10,000 account

Over the full year (MT5, 1 lot) this EA had 27 losing streaks of 4+ trades (longest: 11), 29 of 184 trading days as bad as yesterday, a worst day of −$3,782 and a worst drawdown of $12,681. At 2 lots those become a worst day of −$7,563 and a drawdown of $25,361 — 254% of a $10,000 account. Every stop here cost $1,500–2,200 (15–22% of the account). The strategy is profitable over the year, but at this size its ordinary bad stretches empty the account first.

One-off or a pattern? The full-year check

Full year: setups with chop under 0.15 made 166 trades and +$52,087 — low chop is not a losing category on its own. BUYs with no TP1 ("path B"): 281 trades, 38% won, +$77,462 — they win less often but their winners run far.

What would have prevented it

No single tested rule removes this kind of trade without costing more over the year (report.md §10, §12). The honest lever is the size: at the designed 0.4 lots for this account the loss would have been about −$362.
